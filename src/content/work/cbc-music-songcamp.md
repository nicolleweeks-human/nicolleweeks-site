I co-created SongCamp, a CBC Music series that brought some of Canada's brightest YouTube music creators into the CBC studio to collaborate — and became the network's first YouTube-to-TV crossover.

## The format

In 2014, YouTube was where a generation of Canadian musicians was already building an audience. Television hadn't caught up. SongCamp was the bridge: invite the creators who'd built followings on their own terms, put them in a real studio together, and let the collaboration be the show. The output lived natively on YouTube and on CBC's broadcast feed at the same time — one piece of work, two platforms, no translation tax.

That dual-home format was new for CBC. It meant rethinking how a music show got conceived, shot, and released so it could earn attention in both places without feeling like a repurpose of either.

## My role

Co-creator. That's the credit that matters here, and it's the one that the original page buried. I helped shape what SongCamp was — the concept, the format, the editorial frame for who got invited and why — not only how it got made. Co-creator means the show existed because a small group of us argued it into existence inside a public broadcaster that hadn't done anything quite like it before.

## Why it still matters

Cross-platform creative leadership is now table stakes for almost every content role. In 2014 it wasn't. Convincing a national broadcaster to meet creators on the platform they'd already chosen — instead of asking them to translate themselves onto TV — was the harder, more interesting half of the job. It's the same instinct I bring to every channel decision I make now: go to where the audience already is, and build the format around that, not the other way around.
