Most style guides die in a Confluence page no one opens. This one didn't, because it wasn't built as a rulebook — it was built as a shared system that five business lines helped govern, maintain, and use in their day-to-day work. The Scotia Digital Style Guide launched in 2022 and is still in active use, currently evolving into a full content design system.

## Context

Scotiabank's digital content was scattered. Product, comms, and ops teams were writing in different tones, structuring CTAs differently, and applying their own conventions to the same UI patterns. The friction showed up everywhere: in design reviews that relitigated the same decisions, in handoffs that stalled on last-minute rewrites, and — most importantly — in journeys where customers hit one voice in onboarding and a completely different one three screens later.

There was no shortage of opinions about what good content looked like. What was missing was a shared system anyone could point to, contribute to, and trust.

## The problem

A bank the size of Scotiabank doesn't have one content team. It has many, across product lines that each have their own pressures, partners, and history. A top-down style guide written in isolation would have been ignored within a quarter. So would a guide that ducked the hard conventions and left every team to interpret on their own.

The work had to do three things at once:

- Create real consistency across journeys customers move between
- Resolve genuinely conflicting stakeholder preferences without flattening anyone's expertise
- Be light enough to maintain that contributors actually kept it current

## The approach

I initiated and led the creation of the Scotia Digital Style Guide — a centralized library of CTAs, tone principles, and UI content patterns used across customer journeys. But the artifact was only half the project. The other half was the governance model that kept it alive.

I treated the guide as a product with users (the writers and designers shipping flows every week) rather than a policy document with subjects. That reframing changed everything about how it got built.

## What I built

**A centralized pattern library, grounded in real product examples.** The guide pulled CTAs, microcopy, and content structures from work already in production. Teams could apply it without training because they recognized the examples — these were screens they'd shipped or were about to.

**The Content Community of Practice (CCoP).** A standing group of contributors from five business lines who help maintain and govern the system. The CCoP gave the guide legitimacy it couldn't have earned on its own. Decisions came out of the room, not down from one team.

**A governance model designed to be sustainable.** Monthly syncs, version control, and an intake process that pulled updates directly from active projects. New patterns surfaced through real work instead of through a separate "guide team" trying to predict what writers would need.

**Working sessions across all five business lines** to align on voice and tone, surface the genuine conflicts, and resolve them with the people who would have to live with the outcome.

## A representative call

One business unit pushed back hard on changing their CTA structure. Their language had history, internal advocates, and a clear point of view about what their brand sounded like.

Instead of arguing brand versus brand, I mapped their CTA language to the user confusion patterns we'd already documented — moments where customers hesitated, tapped the wrong thing, or bounced. The conversation shifted from preference to evidence. The decision moved from branding to clarity, and the change landed without bruising the relationship.

That's the kind of negotiation a governance model has to make space for. A guide that can't survive disagreement doesn't survive at all.

## The shift in how the team worked

Before the guide, content lived in scattered Figma comments, Word docs, and last-minute rewrites pulled together under deadline. After, writers and designers could work in parallel — designers building flows against established patterns, writers refining at the edges instead of starting from scratch every time.

## Outcomes

- **Faster production timelines**, with reusable components teams could grab without negotiating from zero
- **A visible reduction in tone and style inconsistencies** across journeys, especially at the handoffs between business lines
- **Active use across product, design, and comms**, with governance carried by ongoing CCoP rituals rather than a single owner
- **A foundation that's still evolving** — the guide is currently being expanded into a full content design system

## Why this worked

The line I keep coming back to: we didn't write rules, we built a system people wanted to use. When consistency is easier than improvising, the system stops being a tax and starts being a tool. That's the threshold a content design system has to cross to matter.

## Reflection

The hardest part of this work wasn't the writing or the taxonomy. It was the politics — five business lines, real conflicts of opinion, and the constant temptation to either centralize too hard or decentralize into incoherence. The CCoP held the middle. It gave contributors a seat at the table, which is the only reason they showed up to maintain the work after launch.

If I were starting again, I'd invest earlier in measurement — a clearer baseline on inconsistency and review cycles before the guide existed, so the after-picture had sharper numbers to point to. The qualitative shift was unmistakable to anyone working in the system. A tighter quantitative story would have made it easier to evangelize beyond it.
